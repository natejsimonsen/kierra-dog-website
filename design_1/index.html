The Story

From K9 Care to K9 Training

In 2003, Kierra opened her business under a different name — Kierra’s K9 Care — with little more than a leash, a pocket of treats, and a conviction that dogs are more legible than most people give them credit for. Two decades of hands-on work, continuing education, and a growing list of certifications later, that small practice has become Kierra’s K9 Training: personalized, science-based coaching built to strengthen the whole relationship, not just the sit-stay.

She doesn’t start with commands. She starts with posture, ears, tail carriage, the half-second before a bark — the vocabulary dogs already speak fluently, whether or not we’re listening for it. Behavior has a reason behind it: fear, overstimulation, an unmet need, a skill that was never taught. Find the reason, and the training plan tends to write itself.

I don’t just teach commands. I work to understand why the behavior is happening at all.— Kierra

That’s the difference between a trainer who suppresses a behavior and one who changes it. Training, done this way, looks less like a drill and more like a shared language — one dogs and their people build together, on both ends of the leash. When a dog enjoys participating, training stops being a chore and starts being quality time.

Since those early days as Kierra’s K9 Care, the practice has kept expanding — through hands-on experience, professional courses, conferences, and ongoing study in dog behavior. The name changed. The habit of asking why first never did.

Method

Positive, on purpose

Every session runs on modern learning science: clear markers, real rewards, and a relationship built on trust instead of pressure. Food, toys, praise, and a clicker’s precise timing tell a dog exactly which choice just paid off — which makes the next right choice easier to find.

Clicker training works because it’s honest. The click means the same thing every time: that’s it, that’s the one. No guessing, no correction, no confusion about what’s being asked — just information a dog can act on, and a training process that feels more like a game than a test.

What this practice does not use
  • Shock or electronic collars
  • Choke collars
  • Prong collars
  • Fear, pain, force, or intimidation
